Hawaii has often dodged the path of hurricanes. Why did it see so many storms this summer?

Hurricane Lowell surged near the Hawaiian Islands this week, leaving a trail of downed trees and power lines, crumbled roads, sunken boats and destroyed piers on the island of Kauai. Residents were left to grapple not only with the damage, but the rising risks to the tropical archipelago as sea surface temperatures climb. Despite its location, stretching across roughly 1,500 miles in the central north Pacific Ocean, Hawaii typically avoids the worst effects from hurricanes. In fact, the island chain so often dodges direct hits from the violent storms, that it’s been quipped they have a force field around them. But Lowell was the second named storm to brush by the islands so far this year. In August, Hurricane Lala hit the Big Island and Oahu with damaging winds, the first time hurricane-force gusts have been recorded there in 155 years. “An event of this magnitude is somewhat rare,” Michael Brennan, director of the National Hurricane Center said during a recent broadcast discussion on Lowell’s forecast, adding that Hawaii has not had a direct hurricane landfall since Iniki in 1992. That violent storm unleashed 145 mile-per-hour winds as it tore through Kauai, destroying or damaging roughly 70% of the island’s homes and businesses. It’s not luck (or a force field) that typically protected the islands, but location, according to Dr Nicholas Grondin, assistant professor of Environmental Science at the University of Tampa. Among the most isolated chain of islands on Earth, Hawaii is simply a small target in the vast open ocean, Grondin explained in an editorial published by the Conversation. Most approaching storms also must pass over cooler water temperatures to the east of the islands, which helps calm them. “Without warm oceans to provide fuel, hurricanes tend to weaken and fizzle out as they approach the islands,” Grondin said. Sea-surface temperatures in the tropical Pacific are, however, exceptionally warm this year due to El Niño . The climate phenomenon has already developed, but is expected to strengthen through the coming months typically limits hurricane activity in the Atlantic and enhances the development and strength of storms in the Pacific. Forecasters have warned it could reach a historic intensity. El Niño has caused hurricanes to form further to the south and west this year, according to Matthew Rosencrans, Noaa’s lead forecaster for the seasonal hurricane outlook, which “puts them in a place where they could make that westward run towards the Hawaiian islands”. It also makes for a lagging storm season. Hurricane risks typically extend from July to the end of November but fall off into the later months. September and October are far busier in El Niño years. The climate crisis has also played a part in strengthening the storms. The effects of El Niño are layered on top of the warming trends. Hurricane Lowell temporarily became a category 5 on 2 September. It swept over waters that were unusually warm, which helped fuel its ferocity. The sea surface temperatures along its path were “made up to 60 times more likely because of human-caused climate change”, according to a report from Climate Central, an organization of scientists and advocates who research impacts from global heating. Together, the climate crisis and El Niño are expected to supercharge extreme weather events around the world over the next year. For Pacific storms, Rosencrans said, it means they may carry more moisture and produce stronger winds. As efforts begin to clear debris and repair roads and infrastructure damaged during Lowell, emergency management officials must continue keeping close watch on the waters for the next big storm. “They just need to reset and regard any potential upcoming impacts,” Rosencrans said. “The season is not over – there is still a long bit to go.” The island chain has tended to avoid the worst effects, but Hurricanes Lowell and Lala left a trail of damage this year

夏威夷经常躲过飓风的路径。为何今年夏天遭遇如此多风暴？

本周，飓风“洛厄尔”在夏威夷群岛附近肆虐，给考艾岛留下了一连串倒下的树木和电线、破碎的道路、沉没的船只和被毁坏的码头。 居民们不仅要应对损失，还要应对随着海面温度上升，这个热带群岛面临的风险不断上升的问题。 尽管夏威夷横跨中北太平洋约1500英里，但它通常能避开飓风带来的最严重影响。事实上，这个岛链经常避开暴风雨的直接袭击，以至于有人打趣说，它们周围有防护罩。 但“洛厄尔”是今年到目前为止第二个经过这些岛屿的风暴。今年8月，飓风“拉拉”以破坏性的大风袭击了大岛和瓦胡岛，这是155年来首次在那里记录到飓风强度的阵风。 “这种规模的事件有些罕见，”美国国家飓风中心主任迈克尔·布伦南在最近一次关于“洛厄尔”预报的广播讨论中说。他补充说，自1992年“伊尼基”以来，夏威夷还没有飓风直接登陆过。那场猛烈的风暴以每小时145英里的风速席卷考艾岛，摧毁或破坏了该岛约70%的房屋和企业。 据坦帕大学环境科学助理教授尼古拉斯·格隆丁博士称，夏威夷群岛通常受到保护并非靠运气（或力场），而是靠地理位置。格隆丁在《对话》杂志发表的一篇评论中解释说，夏威夷是地球上最孤立的岛链之一，在广阔的海洋中只是一个小目标。大多数接近夏威夷的风暴还必须经过岛屿东部较冷的海水，这有助于风暴减弱。格隆丁说：“如果没有温暖的海洋提供能量，飓风在接近岛屿时往往会减弱并逐渐消失。”然而，由于厄尔尼诺现象，今年热带太平洋的海面温度异常温暖。这种气候现象已经形成，但预计将在未来几个月加强，通常会限制大西洋的飓风活动，并增强太平洋风暴的发展和强度。预报员警告说，它可能达到历史性的强度。美国国家海洋和大气管理局季节性飓风展望首席预报员马修·罗森克兰斯表示，厄尔尼诺现象导致今年飓风在更南部和西部形成，这“使它们能够向西移动，朝向夏威夷群岛”。这也导致风暴季节滞后。飓风风险通常从7月持续到11月底，但在接下来的几个月里会减弱。在厄尔尼诺现象出现的年份，9月和10月要繁忙得多。 气候危机也在风暴增强中发挥了作用。厄尔尼诺现象的影响叠加在变暖趋势之上。 飓风“洛厄尔”于9月2日短暂达到五级强度。它扫过异常温暖的海域，这助长了它的凶猛程度。据气候中心的一份报告称，其路径上的海面温度“因人为导致的气候变化而出现的概率增加了多达60倍”。气候中心是一个由研究全球变暖影响的科学家和倡导者组成的组织。 气候危机和厄尔尼诺现象叠加在一起，预计将在未来一年加剧全球各地的极端天气事件。罗森克兰斯说，对于太平洋风暴而言，这意味着它们可能携带更多水汽并产生更强风力。 随着清理废墟和修复“洛厄尔”期间受损道路和基础设施的工作展开，应急管理官员必须继续密切关注海域情况，以应对下一场大风暴。 “他们只需要重新调整状态，并重视任何可能即将到来的影响，”罗森克兰斯说，“这个季节还没有结束——还有很长一段时间。” 这个岛链历来能够避开最严重的影响，但今年飓风“洛厄尔”和“拉拉”留下了一路破坏。
